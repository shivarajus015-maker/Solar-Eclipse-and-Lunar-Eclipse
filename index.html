Physics & Geometry

How Shadows Create Eclipses

An extended light source like the Sun casts three distinct shadow regions behind an opaque moon or planet.

Inner Cone

Umbra (Total Shadow)

The darkest central cone where all direct sunlight is completely blocked. Observers inside the Moon's umbra experience a Total Solar Eclipse; when the Moon enters Earth's umbra, a Total Lunar Eclipse occurs.

Outer Cone

Penumbra (Partial Shadow)

The wider outer region where only part of the Sun is obscured. Being inside the penumbra produces a Partial Solar Eclipse or a subtle Penumbral Lunar Eclipse.

Beyond Umbra Tip

Antumbra ("Ring of Fire")

When the Moon is near Apogee (farther from Earth), its umbra ends before reaching Earth's surface. Observers in the diverging antumbra see a bright golden ring around the Moon — an Annular Solar Eclipse.

Fascinating Eclipse Science

  • Why Not Every Month? The Moon's orbit is tilted by 5.14° to Earth's orbital plane (the ecliptic). Most New and Full Moons pass slightly above or below the shadow cone unless near an orbital node.
  • Why Is a Total Lunar Eclipse Red? Earth's atmosphere scatters short blue wavelengths (Rayleigh scattering) and bends longer red-orange light into the umbra — projecting every sunrise and sunset on Earth onto the Moon!
  • The Cosmic Coincidence (400× Ratio) The Sun is about 400× wider than the Moon, and also about 400× farther from Earth — making both appear ~0.5° wide in our sky.
